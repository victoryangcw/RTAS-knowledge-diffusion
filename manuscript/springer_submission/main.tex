\abstract{Universities often expect faculty research to inform undergraduate training, but whether students' project topics are close to the research conducted in their academic units is rarely measured directly. We introduce the Research--Training Alignment Score (RTAS), a project-level measure of the semantic similarity between a project title and the titles of articles linked to the project's academic unit through advisor--author matching. At Wuhan University, RTAS is defined for 3,713 of the 3,714 registered undergraduate innovation and entrepreneurship training projects; the project frame is retained, and the one project without a matched reference portfolio is examined in a full-frame sensitivity analysis. The study also uses 56,901 OpenAlex articles published in 2020--2024, of which 33,312 are assigned to at least one academic unit and contribute to RTAS reference portfolios. In a human-rating check of 150 project--article title pairs, embedding similarity was associated with judged relatedness (Spearman's $\rho=0.405$; area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) $=0.880$); additional checks used independent raters, repeated ratings, a large-language-model rater, direct lexical baselines, and a second embedding model. About three quarters of RTAS variance lies between academic units (intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) $=0.74$). After accounting for unit and advisor characteristics, administrative-tier coefficients are not distinguishable from zero. Advisors' publication activity in the three years before a project is positively associated with RTAS, whereas their previously observed supervisory load is negatively associated; both associations persist after the focal advisors' own papers are removed from the reference portfolios. National-level project allocation is concentrated (Gini $=0.746$) and shows year-to-year persistence (odds ratio (OR) $=2.06$), but projects linked to the top 5\% of advisors by national-project count show no clear alignment advantage (Cohen's $d=0.083$, $p=0.067$). Topic-prevalence patterns change when the article and project samples are balanced, and annual onset can be identified on both sides for only 29 of 886 topics. RTAS is intended to measure semantic proximity between project content and its surrounding research environment, not research quality, teaching effectiveness, or causal effects.}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}

Universities often expect active research to enrich undergraduate learning. Yet the presence of a strong research environment does not, by itself, show that students' work is connected to the research taking place around them. The relationship between research and teaching has long been debated. A widely cited meta-analysis found only a weak overall relationship between individual research productivity and teaching effectiveness, suggesting that the connection cannot be understood simply by comparing broad measures of research and teaching performance \citep{hattie1996relationship}. Later work has therefore examined the different ways research and teaching may be connected in particular educational settings \citep{brew2003teaching,healey2005linking,linn2015undergraduate}.

One part of this relationship can be examined by looking at what students actually work on. In faculty-supervised undergraduate projects, students develop a research question, solve a practical problem, or explore a proposed topic with guidance from academic staff. However, the subject matter of an individual project is not necessarily close to the research being conducted in the academic unit where it is supervised. Existing studies have more often compared research and teaching through indicators measured at the level of individual scholars or institutions \citep{maisano2023empirical,scafetta2025rt,xiong2026nexus}. These studies help describe relationships between research and teaching performance, but they do not directly show whether the content of a particular student project is related to the publication portfolio of its academic environment.

This is also a measurement challenge. Scientometric research has used citations, co-citations, bibliographic coupling, co-word analysis, and publication classifications to describe relationships among scholarly documents \citep{small1973cocitation,callon1991coword,boyack2010cocitation,yanding2012networks,waltman2012classification}. More recent work uses text representations to identify similarities in meaning, including relationships that are not visible from citations or shared words alone \citep{lamers2021appraisal,huang2022interdisciplinarity,motohashi2024measuring}. Such approaches are particularly relevant when the records being compared use different languages. In the present study, Chinese characters account for 92.3\% of project-title characters on average, whereas 56,858 of the 56,901 OpenAlex article titles (99.9\%) contain no Chinese characters. A comparison based only on shared words or characters would therefore miss many potential relationships between the two sets of titles.

We examine this question using Wuhan University's Undergraduate Innovation and Entrepreneurship Training Program (UIETP).\footnote{The UIETP is a Chinese undergraduate project scheme that supports both innovation-training and entrepreneurship-training/practice projects. Official project-type labels are available for 2021--2024 and identify 158 entrepreneurship-track projects in the analytic sample. Excluding these projects leaves the substantive mixed-model conclusions unchanged. The 2020 archive does not provide project-type labels.} Projects are supervised by faculty members and approved at university, provincial, or national administrative tiers. The program's administrative structure is specific to the Chinese higher-education context, and we do not assume that it is identical to undergraduate research programs elsewhere. The broader measurement question, however, is not unique to China: universities in different systems may support faculty-supervised research projects, capstone projects, or other forms of undergraduate inquiry. Where project descriptions and publication records are available, the same approach can be adapted to examine how student work relates to the research environment around it.

\subsection{This study}

We introduce the Research--Training Alignment Score (RTAS), which measures the semantic similarity between an individual project title and the titles of articles in the publication portfolio associated with the project's academic unit. Articles are linked to academic units through the names of project advisors and article authors. For each project, RTAS is the mean cosine similarity between its multilingual sentence embedding and the embeddings of the unique articles in the relevant unit portfolio over a cumulative publication window.

RTAS is deliberately narrower than a measure of project or institutional performance. It describes how closely a project's title relates in meaning to the observed publication portfolio around it. It does not measure whether a project is novel, scientifically important, well supervised, or educationally successful, and it is not designed to support causal conclusions or performance rankings.

Throughout the paper, \emph{academic unit} is used as an umbrella term for the schools, colleges, institutes, laboratories, and other organizational units represented in the project registry. The source-data field and analysis code retain the historical label \texttt{college}.

We ask four research questions about the 2020--2024 Wuhan University cohorts:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1.}] \textbf{Differences across administrative tiers.} Does RTAS differ across university-, provincial-, and national-level projects? Do these differences remain after accounting for academic-unit and advisor characteristics?
\item[\textbf{RQ2.}] \textbf{Topic prevalence and timing.} How are research-side and project-side topic prevalence distributed across the four high/low combinations? For topics that reach the annual presence threshold on both sides, which side reaches it first?
\item[\textbf{RQ3.}] \textbf{Academic-unit and advisor associations.} How much of the variation in RTAS lies between academic units, and how are advisor and project characteristics associated with project-level alignment?
\item[\textbf{RQ4.}] \textbf{Concentration and persistence.} How concentrated is the allocation of national-level projects across advisors, how often does such allocation recur in the following year, and does advisor concentration correspond to higher RTAS among the projects they supervise?
\end{enumerate}

Studying one university holds the broad institutional setting and observation period constant while allowing comparisons across 40 academic units and different fields. This helps describe variation within the university, but the results should not automatically be generalized to other institutions (Sect.~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\subsection{Contributions}

The main contribution is methodological. RTAS provides a project-level way to compare the content of undergraduate projects with the publication portfolios of the academic units in which those projects are supervised, including settings where project and article titles are written in different languages. The study examines the embedding measure through human ratings, agreement between raters, repeated ratings, a large-language-model second rater, direct lexical comparisons, and a second-model benchmark. It also makes the choice of how article similarities are combined explicit and keeps that choice fixed in the final analysis.

We then use RTAS to examine four aspects of the research--training relationship: differences across administrative project tiers, topic-prevalence patterns and the limited number of observable temporal lags, variation across academic units and advisor characteristics, and the concentration and year-to-year persistence of national-level project allocation. These analyses demonstrate how the measure can be used; they are not intended to identify causal effects. Figure~\ref{fig:roadmap} summarizes the data sources and analytical steps.

\section{Data and Methods}

\subsection{Data sources and corpus tiers}

\textbf{Projects.} The project dataset contains 3,714 projects approved under Wuhan University's Undergraduate Innovation and Entrepreneurship Training Program (UIETP) between 2020 and 2024: 1,375 university-level (37.0\%), 1,657 provincial-level (44.6\%), and 682 national-level (18.4\%). The records were provided by the university's teaching administration. The fields used in this study correspond to the approval information publicly released by the university each year and were consolidated into a single study dataset. Each project record contains its title, administrative tier, year, academic unit, and advisor information; student names were not used. Chinese characters account for 92.3\% of project-title characters on average. After multi-advisor fields were split using the same delimiters and name-normalization rules used to construct advisor covariates, the dataset contained 1,834 distinct advisors and 4,244 distinct advisor--project links. Each link represents one advisor attached to one project; it is not an additional project count. A total of 528 projects have more than one advisor.

The program includes both innovation-training and entrepreneurship-training/practice projects. Archived approval lists provide explicit project-type labels for 2021--2024, but not for 2020. Re-parsing the available lists identified 158 entrepreneurship-training/practice projects in the analytic sample (4.3\%). Excluding these projects leaves every primary mixed-model coefficient with the same direction and significance classification (Online Resource 1, Table~S1). The archived 2022 list used in this study contains no university-level project records. This does not establish that no such projects existed across the university that year; excluding the entire 2022 cohort leaves the RQ1 and mixed-model conclusions unchanged (Online Resource 1, Table~S2).

\textbf{Study frame.} The five-year registry initially contained 3,887 rows under 48 organizational labels. RTAS requires a non-empty publication portfolio linked to the academic unit through project-advisor and article-author names. For 173 projects under eight organizational labels, the available records did not allow such a portfolio to be constructed. These projects were therefore outside the RTAS analytic frame, which contains 3,714 projects across 40 academic units. Checks conducted after the data were frozen confirmed that all 3,714 project identifiers are unique, that no normalized project title is repeated within the same year, and that the seven titles appearing in more than one year refer to separately approved continuing projects rather than duplicate records.

\textbf{Articles.} We retrieved 56,901 OpenAlex records of type \texttt{article} for Wuhan University (OpenAlex institution ID I37461747) and publication years 2020--2024 through cursor pagination on September 3, 2026. Of these titles, 56,858 (99.9\%) contain no Chinese characters. Annual counts were 9,970, 10,117, 12,002, 11,954, and 12,858. The retrieval files did not retain OpenAlex's language field, so we describe the titles by their character content rather than treating all of them as verified English-language titles. OpenAlex provides an open infrastructure for scholarly metadata \citep{priem2022openalex}; recent comparisons have found strong reference coverage while also documenting limitations that differ across bibliographic databases \citep{culbert2025openalex}. We also retrieved 22,438 articles published in 2017--2019 on September 4, 2026. These earlier articles bring the total number of retrieved records across both periods to 79,339, but they serve only to calculate advisors' publication counts before each project began. They do not enter RTAS reference portfolios or the topic model.

\textbf{Advisor--article linkage.} Chinese advisor names were romanized into Pinyin keys and compared with normalized OpenAlex author names. To allow for differences in how names are ordered in Latin-script publication records, we generated keys in both surname--given-name and given-name--surname order, as well as a shorter key combining the surname with the first syllable of the given name. These alternative keys help accommodate name-order differences, but they can also cause two different people with similar romanized names to share a key.

Two separate ambiguity rules were used for two different tasks. For the article-to-unit map, a name match was treated as ambiguous and not assigned when the matching advisors belonged to more than one academic unit. For the project-level advisor publication covariate, an advisor was flagged as ambiguous if any of the keys generated from that advisor's name also matched another advisor. This flag did not automatically discard every paper match for that advisor: the advisor could still be linked to papers through keys that did not collide with another name. A project was classified as matched (2,750) if at least one advisor with linked papers had no key collision, ambiguous (748) if all paper-bearing advisors had at least one key collision, and unmatched (216) if no advisor could be linked to a paper. Of the 748 ambiguous-status projects, 481 nevertheless retained a usable publication count through non-colliding keys and entered the primary complete-case model ($n=3{,}231$); the remaining 267 ambiguous projects and all 216 unmatched projects had no usable publication count and were excluded. The high-confidence-match sensitivity analysis includes the 2,750 matched projects and excludes all ambiguous-status projects.

For advisors with no linked article records, the publication covariate was coded as missing rather than zero. A failed name match does not establish that an advisor published no articles; it means that the available records did not support a reliable link. These missing values were therefore left out of the across-advisor mean rather than treated as observed zero publication counts.

\textbf{Three data collections.} The article retrieval dataset contains all 56,901 articles and provides the full denominator for article-side topic prevalence. The advisor-matched academic-unit portfolios contain 33,312 articles and are the only articles used to calculate RTAS. The joint BERTopic corpus contains 60,615 titles: all 56,901 article titles and all 3,714 project titles.

\subsection{RTAS definition}

For academic unit $u$, year $t$, and project $p$, RTAS is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RTAS}(p,u,t)=
\frac{1}{|P_{u,t}|}
\sum_{j\in P_{u,t}}
\cos(\mathbf e_p,\mathbf e_j),
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf e_p$ is the 384-dimensional embedding of the project title and $\mathbf e_j$ is the embedding of article $j$. Both vectors are scaled to unit length using L2 normalization before cosine similarity is calculated. The embedding model that produces these 384-dimensional vectors is described in Sect.~2.3. $P_{u,t}$ is the set of unique articles assigned to academic unit $u$ over the cumulative period from 2020 through year $t$, using advisor--author linkage. It is a set of articles, not a probability. If an article is linked to several advisors within the same academic unit, it is counted only once in that unit's portfolio. If it is linked to advisors in different academic units, it is counted once in each relevant unit. RTAS is the average of the project title's cosine similarities with all articles in the relevant portfolio.

A non-empty article portfolio is required to calculate RTAS. In the frozen analytic frame, 3,713 of the 3,714 projects have a non-empty advisor-linked portfolio and a defined RTAS. For one project, no article could be linked to its advisor, leaving its reference portfolio empty; a similarity score therefore cannot be calculated. This project remains in the 3,714-project frame, but its RTAS is treated as undefined in the primary analysis. The primary RTAS analyses use the 3,713 defined values. A separate full-frame sensitivity analysis assigns RTAS = 0 to this one project so that the effect of retaining it in the numerical comparison can be examined.

We use titles because the project records contain no narrative description or abstract beyond the project title. The title is therefore the only text available in a comparable form on both sides. OpenAlex provides abstracts for some articles, but the project records do not contain corresponding abstracts. Comparing project titles with longer article abstracts would introduce a systematic difference in text length and information content, making it harder to interpret the resulting similarity as a comparison of subject matter. RTAS therefore measures how closely a project title relates to the observed advisor-linked publication portfolio of its academic unit. It does not measure novelty, influence, teaching effectiveness, or research quality. Citation counts and journal tiers do not enter the score, although advisors' pre-project publication counts are included as separate covariates.

RTAS also addresses a different question from scholar-level research--teaching composites such as the RT-score \citep{scafetta2025rt}. Those measures combine research and teaching performance at the level of an individual scholar, whereas RTAS compares the content of an individual student project with the publication portfolio of its academic unit. It is a project-level measure of semantic alignment, not a performance ranking.

\subsection{Embedding and aggregation choice}

We used \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2}, which returns a 384-dimensional numerical vector for each title. The vector represents the title in a learned semantic space, allowing titles with related meanings to be compared even when they use different languages. The model belongs to the multilingual Sentence-BERT family. Its Siamese sentence-embedding architecture processes texts through the same model so that their resulting vectors can be compared directly; multilingual knowledge distillation helps place texts from different languages in a shared representation space \citep{reimers2019sbert,reimers2020multilingual}. We selected this model for its multilingual coverage and computational efficiency on the 60,615-title joint dataset. The validity of the resulting title similarities was assessed separately below.

A smaller development set of 12,000 articles was used only to compare five ways of summarizing the similarities between a project and its academic-unit publication portfolio. It was not the final analysis dataset. The five alternatives were: the mean similarity across all articles; the mean similarity across the five, ten, or twenty most similar articles (Top-5, Top-10, and Top-20); and a centroid comparison, which compares the project vector with the average vector of the article portfolio. These alternatives answer slightly different questions: the overall mean reflects similarity to the portfolio as a whole, whereas Top-K gives greater weight to the closest articles.

The alternatives were evaluated using a pre-specified composite rule. The normalized composite gave a weight of 0.45 to separation between known project groups (project-level ANOVA $\eta^2$), 0.30 to stability over time, and 0.25 to stability in the number of topics identified by the topic model. A Top-K method would be preferred to the overall mean only if its composite score reached at least 95\% of the best score. The overall mean scored 0.742, compared with 0.480 for the best Top-K alternative. We therefore selected mean aggregation as the primary RTAS method. This choice was fixed before the final analysis and applied without re-tuning to the full 56,901-article dataset. Because administrative-tier separation contributed to the development-stage choice, the tier comparisons reported below are descriptive rather than independent validation.

For the final dataset, the frozen RTAS values have a mean of 0.1338, a standard deviation of 0.0723, a median of 0.1362, and a range from $-0.0575$ to 0.3960 ($N=3{,}713$). Table~\ref{tab:selection} summarizes the aggregation choice.

\subsection{Validity program}

The primary validation procedures were specified before the main analyses and did not change the frozen RTAS values. The BGE-M3 comparison was added after the main procedure had been fixed. It provides an additional comparison between embedding models but does not replace or alter the primary RTAS calculation.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pairwise semantic validity (C1, embedding level).} We examined whether the similarity scores produced by the embedding model corresponded to human judgments about whether two titles concerned related subjects. A reference rater scored 150 randomly selected project-title--article-title pairs on a 1--4 relatedness scale. A total of 134 pairs received a score of 1. Because the pairs were randomly selected rather than chosen as known matches, this imbalance describes the validation sample; it should not be read as an estimate of how often undergraduate projects are unrelated to university research. Embedding cosine similarity was associated with the human ratings ($\rho=0.405$, $p=2.6\times10^{-7}$). A 10,000-resample bootstrap (seed 42) gave a 95\% confidence interval (CI) of [0.281, 0.509]; the permutation test gave $p<.001$, Kendall's $\tau_b=0.327$, and the largest change in $\rho$ when one pair was removed was 0.018. When ratings were grouped as related ($\geq2$) or unrelated (=1), the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was 0.880 [0.793, 0.949]. These results support the use of the embeddings for comparing individual title pairs, but they do not establish the full construct validity of aggregated RTAS. Only 16 pairs were rated as related; this subset has a narrow range of ratings and is too small for a stable separate correlation estimate. Its $\rho=-0.253$ is reported for transparency and is not used for inference.

\item \textbf{Agreement between human raters.} We summarized agreement on the ordered 1--4 ratings using quadratic-weighted Cohen's $\kappa$, which takes the size of rating differences into account \citep{cohen1968weighted}. Two independent raters, who did not see the reference ratings or embedding scores, rated a subset of 50 pairs. Rater A's agreement with the reference rater was $\kappa=0.465$ (72\% exact agreement); Rater B's was $\kappa=0.606$ (78\%). The reference rater assigned a score of 1 to 46 of the 50 pairs, leaving little variation for a stable Spearman correlation, so agreement is summarized mainly with $\kappa$ and the exact-match rate. Agreement between Raters A and B was $\kappa=0.826$, $\rho=0.821$, and 90\% exact agreement.

\item \textbf{Repeat-rating reliability.} The reference rater scored 40 of the same pairs again after approximately 12 days, with the pairs presented in a new order. The results were $\kappa=0.643$, $\rho=0.688$ ($p<.001$), and 95\% exact agreement.

\item \textbf{Large-language-model second rater.} We used the API model alias \texttt{deepseek-reasoner} (temperature = 0; prompt version v1.1) as an additional rater for all 150 pairs. It was not given the human ratings or embedding scores. Its ratings agreed with the reference ratings at $\kappa=0.459$, $\rho=0.638$ ($p=1.7\times10^{-18}$), and 93.3\% exact agreement. Comparison with Rater B gave $\rho=0.621$ and AUC $=0.932$. These results provide an additional comparison; they do not replace the reference human ratings or the frozen C1 estimates.

\item \textbf{Direct lexical baselines.} We compared the embedding results with three methods that rely on shared character sequences rather than a learned representation of meaning: character 3--5-gram term frequency--inverse document frequency (TF--IDF) cosine similarity, character 3-gram Jaccard similarity, and BM25 Okapi \citep{robertson2009bm25}. These methods were applied directly to the Chinese project titles and predominantly non-Chinese article titles, without first translating the titles into a common language. Their correlations with human ratings ranged from $\rho=0.296$ to 0.300, and their AUC values ranged from 0.585 to 0.586 (TF--IDF: $\rho=0.300$, AUC $=0.586$; Jaccard: $\rho=0.296$, AUC $=0.585$; BM25: $\rho=0.298$, AUC $=0.585$). All were below the multilingual MiniLM results ($\rho=0.405$, AUC $=0.880$). This comparison supports the use of multilingual semantic embeddings over these direct, untranslated lexical comparisons. It does not establish that MiniLM is better than translation-based methods or other more advanced cross-lingual approaches. For related uses of cosine and Jaccard similarity in scientometric research, see \citet{hamers1989similarity}.

\item \textbf{Post-freeze BGE-M3 comparison.} We used a second embedding model, BGE-M3 \citep{chen2024m3embedding}, to represent the same 150 reference-rated pairs. It yielded $\rho=0.334$ and AUC $=0.811$. In 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples (seed 42), the differences between BGE-M3 and MiniLM were $\Delta\rho=-0.071$ (95\% CI [$-0.187$, 0.035]) and $\Delta$AUC $=-0.069$ (95\% CI [$-0.179$, 0.032]). These intervals include zero, and the results do not show a clear improvement over MiniLM. We therefore retain MiniLM as the primary embedding model.

\item \textbf{Hard-pair validation (post-freeze challenge-set check).} The original 150-pair validation set contained many low-relatedness pairs. To check performance on more difficult comparisons, we created an additional, deliberately enriched sample of 174 unique pairs: 50 randomly selected cross-unit pairs, 50 randomly selected within-unit pairs, 50 pairs with medium MiniLM similarity (cosine 0.3--0.6), and all 24 eligible pairs with high MiniLM similarity (cosine $>0.6$). This enriched sample is referred to as a challenge set because it includes more medium- and high-similarity cases than the original random sample; it is not a representative sample of all possible project--article pairs. Two independent raters, blind to the sampling group, cosine scores, and each other's ratings, scored all 174 pairs in different random orders (seed 42 for Rater A and seed 123 for Rater B). Agreement was high (quadratic-weighted $\kappa=0.872$; 89.1\% exact agreement). The two ratings were averaged to obtain a consensus score. No third rater was asked to resolve disagreements, and no pair differed by more than one scale point. Pairs with a consensus score of at least 2 were classified as related in the binary analysis. Embedding cosine similarity correlated with the consensus ratings at $\rho=0.717$ ($p<0.001$), and AUC was 0.910. Because the pairs were partly selected using MiniLM similarity, these results describe performance on the challenge set rather than validity in the full population of possible pairs. In the high-similarity group ($n=24$; 22 related and 2 unrelated), the descriptive AUC was 0.727 (bootstrap 95\% CI [0.364, 1.000], $B=10{,}000$). The wide interval reflects the small number of pairs and the scarcity of unrelated cases, so this result is only a limited check on difficult examples.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Topic modeling}

We used BERTopic \citep{grootendorst2022bertopic} to group the 60,615 titles in the joint dataset: 56,901 article titles and 3,714 project titles. The model used the same multilingual MiniLM embeddings as RTAS. It first used Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to reduce each 384-dimensional title vector to five dimensions while retaining information about which titles are near one another \citep{mcinnes2018umap}. It then used Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (HDBSCAN) to group titles that occupy dense regions of this reduced space \citep{mcinnes2017hdbscan}. In this context, density refers to how closely documents are grouped in the embedding space: documents in sufficiently populated, closely connected regions can form a topic, whereas isolated documents may remain unassigned as outliers.

The frozen UMAP settings were 15 neighbors, 5 components, minimum distance 0.0, cosine distance, and random seed 42. HDBSCAN used a minimum cluster size of 10, a minimum of 5 samples, and Euclidean distance in the UMAP space. Cluster selection used the Excess of Mass (EOM) rule, which favors clusters that remain stable across different density levels rather than simply splitting the data into the largest possible number of small groups. For each topic, BERTopic reported the 10 most representative terms, using unigrams and bigrams and its multilingual setting. These terms were identified using class-based term frequency--inverse document frequency (c-TF-IDF), which highlights words that are characteristic of one topic relative to the other topics.

HDBSCAN identified 886 non-outlier topics and left 23,318 documents unassigned as outliers (38.47\%); the remaining 37,297 documents were assigned to topics (61.53\%). The quadrant and diffusion-lag analyses therefore concern the 886 identified topics, not the outlier documents. In sensitivity analyses using alternative minimum-cluster-size/minimum-sample settings (15/7, 20/10, and 8/3), the outlier rate remained between 0.364 and 0.396, and the broad quadrant pattern did not change. The number of topics varied from 454 to 1,287 as the settings produced broader or more finely divided clusters, as expected.

\subsection{Analysis windows}

The analyses use different time windows because they address different questions. RTAS uses the cumulative article portfolio from 2020 through the project year, $[2020,t]$. The advisor publication covariate uses the three years before the project year, $[t-3,t-1]$, so that publications from the project year itself are not counted as prior activity. The additional 2017--2019 article records are needed only to calculate this three-year publication history for projects beginning in 2020.

The prior-supervision covariate in RQ3 uses a different window: it counts all projects recorded for an advisor before the focal project's year, rather than only the previous three years. Because the project registry begins in 2020, supervision before that year is not captured. The RQ4 lagged model uses a third, narrower measure: the number of projects an advisor supervised in year $t-1$ alone.

For the temporal topic analysis, a topic was not treated as present merely because it appeared once in a year. It had to meet both a minimum count and a minimum share of that year's documents. On the article side, it had to appear in at least three articles and account for at least 0.2\% of all articles published that year (approximately 20--26 articles per year in 2020--2024). On the project side, it had to appear in at least two projects and account for at least 0.2\% of all projects recorded that year. The first year in which a topic met both conditions was treated as the year it first reached the annual presence threshold. A diffusion lag was calculated only when this year could be identified on both sides. The lag is the project-side year minus the article-side year: a positive value means the topic reached the threshold in articles first, a negative value means the topic reached the threshold in projects first, and zero means it reached the threshold on both sides in the same year.

\subsection{Statistical models}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} We used a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare mean RTAS across university-, provincial-, and national-level projects. In other words, RTAS is the outcome being compared, and administrative tier defines the three groups. Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) tests identify which pairs of group means differ while adjusting for the family of pairwise comparisons ($\Delta=$ higher tier $-$ lower tier). For the largest contrast (national vs university), a Welch unequal-variance $t$ test and Cohen's $d$ are reported as descriptive checks. The 95\% confidence interval (CI) for $\eta^2$ was estimated from 10,000 bootstrap resamples (seed 42). These comparisons are descriptive rather than independent validation because administrative-tier separation also contributed to the earlier choice of aggregation method.

\item \textbf{RQ2.} We compared how widely each topic appeared in the research corpus (articles) and the project dataset. For each topic, article-side prevalence over 2020--2024 was calculated as the number of articles assigned to that topic divided by all 56,901 retrieved article titles. Project-side prevalence was calculated in the same way, using the number of projects assigned to the topic divided by all 3,714 project titles. The denominators include titles that HDBSCAN classified as outliers; these titles remain part of the full dataset but are not counted toward any topic. The two prevalence values are calculated separately and then combined to classify each topic; neither is calculated as a share of the joint corpus. We classified each side as high or low prevalence using the pre-specified cutoffs: at least 1.5\% on the article side and at least 0.500\% on the project side. Combining the two classifications produced four quadrants: HRHT (high prevalence in both articles and projects), HRLT (high in articles but low in projects), LRHT (low in articles but high in projects), and LRLT (low in both). If a fixed cutoff produced fewer than two high-prevalence topics on a side, we used the pre-specified fallback of classifying the highest-prevalence 20\% of topics on that side as high, including all topics tied at the cutoff. In the frozen 886-topic solution, no topic reached the article-side 1.5\% cutoff (about 854 of 56,901 articles), so the fallback was used. This gave an article-side cutoff of 50 articles (0.0879\% of the full article dataset); eight topics were tied at this cutoff (ranks 171--178), so 178 topics were classified as high on the research side. The project-side 0.500\% cutoff (about 19 of 3,714 projects) was retained because 15 topics met it. We also tested article-side thresholds based on the top 15\%, 20\%, and 25\% of topics. Diffusion lags were calculated only for topics that met the annual presence rule on both sides. To test whether the quadrant pattern depended on the difference in dataset sizes (approximately 15 articles for every project), we repeated the same UMAP and HDBSCAN clustering procedure on a balanced dataset containing all 3,714 project titles and a random sample of 3,714 articles (seed 42).

\item \textbf{RQ3.} Following standard multilevel modeling practice \citep{snijders2012multilevel}, we estimated a two-level random-intercept mixed model by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) using \texttt{statsmodels MixedLM} \citep{seabold2010statsmodels}. Projects were nested within academic units (primary complete-case sample $n=3,231$, 39 units; high-confidence-match sensitivity $n=2,750$). The model estimates the overall associations between project characteristics and RTAS while allowing each academic unit to have its own baseline RTAS level. These overall predictor associations are the fixed effects; the unit-specific baseline differences are represented by the random intercepts. Year was entered as a continuous variable centered at 2022. Fixed effects included provincial- and national-level project indicators, the log-transformed number of articles published by the advisor in the three years before the project within the institution-linked OpenAlex dataset, and the log-transformed prior supervision count. The prior supervision count is the cumulative number of projects recorded for the advisor before the focal project's year, averaged across co-advisors; it is not limited to a three-year window. Because the registry begins in 2020, projects supervised before 2020 are not captured, so this measure represents only the supervision history visible in the available registry. An empty model without project-level predictors was used to estimate the null intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Model fit is summarized using marginal $R^2$ (the proportion of variance explained by the fixed effects) and conditional $R^2$ (the proportion explained jointly by fixed and random effects), following \citet{nakagawa2017r2}.

For descriptive context, we also calculated a one-way ANOVA across the 39 academic units with at least two projects; the unit containing only one project was retained only in descriptive summaries. In a post-freeze sensitivity analysis, RTAS was recalculated after removing from each project's academic-unit reference portfolio all articles authored by any of that project's advisors, and the same mixed-model specification was re-estimated. A further sensitivity analysis standardized RTAS within each academic-unit-by-year group: the group mean was subtracted from each project's RTAS and the result divided by that group's standard deviation. This within-unit-year $z$-score expresses a project's position relative to other projects in the same unit and year, rather than its absolute RTAS level. The tier ANOVA and mixed model were then re-estimated using the standardized values and the available complete cases.

Advisor-level dependence was examined separately among single-advisor projects. After restricting the data to complete cases, we combined academic-unit fixed effects with standard errors clustered by advisor; this allows for the possibility that projects supervised by the same advisor have related outcomes. We repeated the clustering using academic-unit-by-normalized-advisor-name identifiers to reduce the risk of treating different people with the same name as one advisor. We also examined a crossed random-intercept model with separate terms for academic units and advisors. This model reached a boundary solution, with the academic-unit variance estimated at zero. Because it did not provide a separately estimated variance for both sources of clustering in this restricted sample, we report it only as a diagnostic check and do not use it for substantive inference.

\item \textbf{RQ4.} Multi-advisor project fields were split using the same delimiter and name-normalization rules as the advisor-covariate construction, yielding 4,244 distinct advisor--project links across 1,834 individual advisors. National-project concentration is summarized using Gini and Pareto measures at the individual-advisor level. We then estimated a lagged logistic regression for whether an advisor received at least one national-level project in year $t$. The model includes whether the advisor received a national-level project in year $t-1$, the number of registered projects supervised in year $t-1$ alone, entered as $\log(1+\mathrm{load}_{t-1})$, and categorical year fixed effects. The year 2021 is the reference because the one-year lag makes it the earliest outcome year. Standard errors are clustered by advisor to account for repeated observations from the same advisor. The primary risk set requires advisors to have projects in both $t-1$ and $t$ (979 advisor-years / 627 advisors); the expanded sensitivity risk set includes advisors active in $t-1$ and treats no project activity in year $t$ as no national-level project (2,469 advisor-years / 1,560 advisors). This design asks whether receiving a national-level project in one year is associated with receiving one again the next year. It avoids defining the top-5\% group from cumulative national-project totals and then using that same full-period group definition to predict national-project receipt.

Separately, each project was classified once as linked to a top-5\% advisor if at least one of its advisors belonged to the top 5\% of individual advisors ranked by cumulative national-project count. These projects were compared descriptively with the remaining projects using a Welch unequal-variance $t$ test and Cohen's $d$. This comparison is not treated as an independent causal or confirmatory test.
\end{itemize}

Statistics within the frozen audit scope were generated from frozen outputs and are rounded for presentation. A post-freeze numerical provenance audit independently recomputed 123 quantities in that frozen scope, and all matched the reported values; the machine-readable verification ledger is available in the public repository. The initial 118-check ledger was subsequently extended by five checks verifying the revised 3,713/3,714 primary-sample conventions. Later additions created after the initial ledger was frozen---including the within-unit-year calibration, balanced-corpus BERTopic sensitivity, and hard-pair challenge set---are identified separately and do not change the 123-check count. Seed 42 was used wherever stochastic procedures required a random seed. Analyses were conducted in Python 3.13; the required Python packages are listed in the repository README.

During manuscript preparation, OpenAI ChatGPT was used to assist with language editing and structural revision. All analyses, numerical results, references, interpretations, and final text were reviewed and verified by the authors.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Administrative project tier and alignment (RQ1)}

Mean RTAS was higher at each successive administrative tier: 0.1213 for university-level projects, 0.1391 for provincial-level projects, and 0.1460 for national-level projects. The overall difference was statistically significant, $F(2,3710)=35.42$, $p=5.8\times10^{-16}$, but its effect size was modest: administrative tier accounted for 1.87\% of the observed RTAS variance ($\eta^2=1.87\%$, 95\% bootstrap CI [1.15\%, 2.86\%]). Tukey comparisons showed higher mean RTAS for provincial- than university-level projects ($\Delta=+0.0178$, $p<0.001$) and for national- than university-level projects ($\Delta=+0.0248$, $p<0.001$). The national--provincial difference was smaller and did not reach statistical significance ($\Delta=+0.0070$, adjusted $p=0.082$). Thus, the three means follow a university--provincial--national ordering, but the data do not establish a difference between the two higher tiers. For the largest contrast, national versus university, the Welch test gave $t=7.350$, $p=3.5\times10^{-13}$, and Cohen's $d=0.351$.

This descriptive gradient was not retained in the adjusted model. After accounting for academic-unit and advisor characteristics, the provincial-level coefficient was close to zero ($\beta=+0.0006$) and the national-level coefficient was also close to zero ($\beta=-0.0016$); both 95\% confidence intervals included zero. The raw differences across tiers therefore provide limited additional information once the academic-unit and advisor context is taken into account. A full-frame sensitivity analysis that assigns RTAS $=0$ to the single project with an empty reference portfolio gives the same substantive pattern: $F(2,3711)=35.72$, $p=4.3\times10^{-16}$, $\eta^2=1.89\%$, and $d=0.352$, compared with $F(2,3710)=35.42$, $p=5.8\times10^{-16}$, $\eta^2=1.87\%$, and $d=0.351$ in the primary sample.

\subsection{Static prevalence quadrants (RQ2, classification)}

The 886 non-outlier BERTopic topics were classified using their pooled 2020--2024 prevalence on the article and project sides. In the full joint corpus, the largest asymmetric group was HRLT: 173 topics had high article-side prevalence but low project-side prevalence. By comparison, 10 topics were LRHT, with low article-side but high project-side prevalence. The other two groups contained five HRHT topics and 698 LRLT topics. The document totals in Table~\ref{tab:quadrants} count only documents assigned to these 886 topics; HDBSCAN outliers are not assigned to a quadrant, although they remain in the full-corpus denominators used to calculate prevalence.

The broad pattern was unchanged when the article-side high-prevalence threshold was varied to the top 15\%, 20\%, or 25\% of topics: HRLT contained 128, 173, and 224 topics, respectively; LRHT contained 10, 10, and 8; and HRHT contained 5, 5, and 7. All 29 topics with definable annual lags remained in HRLT or HRHT under these alternatives. However, the apparent HRLT excess depended on the relative sizes of the two corpora. When the clustering procedure was repeated on a balanced corpus of 3,714 project titles and 3,714 sampled article titles, the 170 resulting topics comprised 9 HRHT, 26 HRLT, 32 LRHT, and 103 LRLT topics. The HRLT group was therefore no longer larger than LRHT (26 versus 32). The 173-versus-10 contrast in the full joint corpus should consequently be interpreted as a descriptive pattern that depends on corpus composition, not as evidence of a stable structural asymmetry between research and training topics. These quadrant labels describe pooled prevalence only; temporal ordering is considered separately and only where annual onset can be identified on both sides.

\subsection{Diffusion lags where definable (RQ2, temporal)}

The annual first-presence rule identified a lag on both sides for only 29 of the 886 topics (3.27\%): 24 HRLT topics and five HRHT topics. Across these 29 topics, the median lag was zero years and the mean was $+0.59$ years. Within HRLT, the median was $+0.5$ years and the mean was $+0.79$ years. Of the 24 HRLT topics, 12 (50.0\%) reached the annual threshold in articles first, nine (37.5\%) reached it in the same year on both sides, and three (12.5\%) reached it in projects first.

For the 10 LRHT topics and 698 LRLT topics, the article-side annual threshold was never reached in any year. Their lags are therefore undefined under this rule; they are not zero-lag topics and are not simply unobserved values. The results support a limited statement about the 29 topics with comparable annual onset, but they do not support a general claim that undergraduate project topics lag behind research. A curriculum or syllabus audit would be a more direct way to investigate whether research developments are subsequently incorporated into formal teaching.

\subsection{Academic-unit context and advisor associations (RQ3)}

The primary random-intercept model included 3,231 complete-case projects from 39 academic units. In the unconditional means model, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.7523; after adding the project- and advisor-level predictors, it remained high at 0.7376. Thus, approximately three quarters of the residual variation in RTAS was between academic units. The model's marginal $R^2$ was 0.0075, compared with a conditional $R^2$ of 0.7395. The measured fixed effects therefore explained only a small share of the variation, while the unit-level random intercepts accounted for a much larger share. A separate descriptive ANOVA across the 39 units with at least two projects gave $F(38,3674)=195.45$, $p<0.001$, and $\eta^2=66.90\%$. This ANOVA effect size and the mixed-model ICC describe different quantities: the former compares unadjusted unit means, whereas the latter estimates the between-unit share of variance within the hierarchical model. The single-project unit is included in descriptive summaries but is not reported separately.

Among the measured project-level predictors, advisor publication activity and prior supervisory load showed the clearest associations with RTAS. The number of articles an advisor published in the three years before the project, within the institution-linked OpenAlex corpus, was positively associated with alignment ($\beta=+0.00427$, $p<0.001$). For scale, the fitted coefficient corresponds to an estimated increase of about 0.0102 RTAS, or 0.14 standard deviations, when the pre-project publication count changes from zero to 10. Prior supervisory load was negatively associated with RTAS ($\beta=-0.00629$, $p=1.9\times10^{-5}$). Year, entered continuously and centered at 2022, was also positively associated with RTAS ($\beta=+0.00313$ per year, $p<0.001$). By contrast, the adjusted provincial- and national-level coefficients were close to zero and their confidence intervals included zero. Because RTAS compares projects with cumulative reference portfolios over $[2020,t]$, later cohorts are evaluated against portfolios that are both larger and compositionally different. The year coefficient should therefore be read as a temporal association under a changing reference set, not as evidence of independent improvement over time.

\textbf{Leave-advisor-out sensitivity.} The primary RTAS portfolio includes publications by the advisors supervising each project, so part of the publication-activity association could arise from this direct overlap. We recalculated RTAS after removing from each project's unit portfolio all articles authored by any of its focal advisors, then re-estimated the same model on the same 3,231 complete cases. The publication coefficient decreased by approximately 16.4\%, from $\beta=+0.00427$ to $\beta=+0.00357$, but remained positive and statistically significant ($p=6.7\times10^{-7}$; 95\% CI [0.00216, 0.00497]). Prior supervisory load also remained negative and significant ($\beta=-0.00653$, $p=8.1\times10^{-6}$; 95\% CI [$-0.00939$, $-0.00366$]). The ICC (0.727) and conditional $R^2$ (0.729) changed little. This sensitivity affected 2,437 of the 3,713 projects with defined RTAS (66\%): on average, 23.6 advisor-authored articles were removed per affected project, corresponding to a mean portfolio reduction of 3.3\%. No project's reference portfolio became empty. The attenuation is consistent with some contribution from direct self-overlap, but most of the publication-activity association remained after those articles were removed.

\textbf{Advisor-dependence sensitivity.} The primary model accounts for clustering within academic units but does not include a separate advisor random effect, even though advisors can supervise multiple projects and 528 projects have more than one advisor. To examine this dependence without duplicating co-supervised projects, we restricted the sensitivity analysis to single-advisor projects: 3,186 projects in total, of which 2,716 had complete covariates; these involved 1,221 advisors, 680 of whom supervised at least two projects. A crossed random-intercept model with separate academic-unit and advisor terms estimated the academic-unit variance at the boundary (zero), so it was not used for substantive inference. We therefore also fitted an alternative with academic-unit fixed effects and standard errors clustered by advisor. The main associations remained: pre-project publications $\beta=+0.0043$ ($p=1.5\times10^{-5}$; 95\% CI [0.0023, 0.0062]), prior supervisory load $\beta=-0.0053$ ($p=0.004$; 95\% CI [$-0.0088$, $-0.0017$]), and year $\beta=+0.0026$ ($p=0.001$); the administrative-tier coefficients remained statistically indistinguishable from zero. Repeating the clustering with academic-unit-by-normalized-advisor-name identifiers, to guard against homonymous names, produced 1,333 clusters rather than 1,221 and virtually identical standard errors and inferential conclusions.

The high-confidence-match sensitivity sample retained the direction and significance classification of every coefficient. Taken together, these analyses show that the advisor associations are robust to the examined linkage and dependence checks, but they remain observational associations and do not establish causal effects. RTAS is also influenced by title length and sparsity, portfolio breadth, organizational composition, and title-language conventions. Academic-unit effects are therefore not interpreted as rankings of unit performance.

\textbf{Within-unit-year calibration sensitivity.} Raw RTAS includes differences in the baseline semantics and breadth of publication portfolios across academic units and years. We therefore standardized RTAS within each academic-unit-by-year cell, expressing each project relative to other projects in the same unit and year. A standardized value could be calculated for 3,709 of the 3,713 projects with defined RTAS. Four projects were the sole RTAS-observed project in their unit-year cell, so a within-cell standard deviation could not be calculated; a fifth singleton cell contained the project with undefined RTAS. The tier ANOVA used the 3,709 standardized projects and showed no tier difference ($F(2,3706)=0.47$, $p=0.62$, $\eta^2=0.0003$). The mixed-model sensitivity used 3,229 complete cases. As expected, the between-unit random-intercept variance reached zero after within-unit-year standardization (ICC $=0.0000$); this is a consequence of the calibration and is not treated as an independent empirical finding. The substantive result is that the advisor associations remained in the same directions and significance classes on the standardized scale: pre-project publications $\beta=+0.0938$ ($p=2.2\times10^{-8}$) and prior supervisory load $\beta=-0.1437$ ($p=2.6\times10^{-5}$), while administrative-tier coefficients remained statistically indistinguishable from zero.

\subsection{Concentration and lagged persistence (RQ4)}

National-level project allocation was concentrated across the 1,834 individual advisors. The national-project Gini coefficient was 0.746, compared with 0.368 for all supervised projects. The national-project calculation includes all 1,834 advisors, including 1,236 who received no national-level project during the observation period. The top 5\% of advisors (92 people) held 31.0\% of national-level projects, while the top 20\% (366 people) held 71.9\%. Overall supervisory activity was also uneven: 818 advisors (44.6\%) supervised exactly one project, whereas 345 (18.8\%) supervised four or more; the mean load was 2.314 projects (median 2; maximum 10).

National-level allocation also showed year-to-year persistence. Among advisors with a registered project in both adjacent years, receiving a national-level project in year $t-1$ was associated with higher odds of receiving one in year $t$ (OR $=2.06$, 95\% CI [1.53, 2.76], $p=1.5\times10^{-6}$; 979 advisor-years from 627 advisors). In the expanded risk set, which includes advisors active in year $t-1$ and treats no project activity in year $t$ as no national-level project, the corresponding estimate was OR $=2.45$ (95\% CI [1.91, 3.16], $p=3.2\times10^{-12}$; 2,469 advisor-years from 1,560 advisors). These are associations between adjacent-year allocation outcomes, not evidence that prior allocation causes subsequent allocation.

The concentration of national-level projects did not translate into a clear RTAS advantage for projects linked to the most frequently funded advisors. Of the 3,713 projects with defined RTAS, 560 had at least one advisor in the top 5\% by cumulative national-project count; these 560 projects are also present in the full 3,714-project registry; the one additional project is the project with undefined RTAS. Compared with the other 3,153 projects with defined RTAS, the top-5\%-exposed projects showed only a small RTAS difference (Cohen's $d=0.083$), which was not statistically significant in the Welch test ($p=0.067$). The results therefore distinguish concentration in project allocation from semantic alignment: advisors who receive a disproportionate share of national projects do not, on these data, show a clear alignment advantage in the projects they supervise.

\section{Discussion}

Raw RTAS exhibits strong between-unit structure: about three quarters of variance lies between academic units. Against this background, the raw gradient across university-, provincial-, and national-level projects becomes very small once academic-unit and advisor characteristics are included in the mixed model. Administrative tier therefore appears to be a weak marker of semantic alignment once institutional context is taken into account. This does not make the raw differences uninformative, but it changes their interpretation: higher-tier projects are observed in different organizational and supervisory environments, and tier itself contributes little additional differentiation after adjustment.

Advisor characteristics point to a related organizational pattern. Projects supervised by advisors with greater recent publication activity in the institution-linked OpenAlex corpus tend to show higher alignment, whereas greater prior supervisory load is associated with lower alignment. Both associations persist after focal-advisor publications are removed from the reference portfolios, with some attenuation in the publication coefficient, and both also persist under within-unit-year standardization. In that calibration, the between-unit variance component reaches zero as expected by construction because RTAS is standardized within academic-unit $\times$ year cells; the ICC collapse is therefore a property of the calibration rather than independent evidence about the data-generating process. The negative load coefficient is compatible with a capacity constraint, but the observational design cannot establish such a mechanism. This interpretation is broadly consistent with mentoring research showing that advisor inputs can matter for trainee outcomes, although \citet{paglis2006adviser} studied doctoral rather than undergraduate training. At the same time, national-level project allocation is highly concentrated and shows adjacent-year persistence, yet projects linked to top-5\% advisors do not show a clear alignment advantage. Concentration in opportunity and semantic alignment therefore should not be treated as interchangeable outcomes. The concentration pattern is consistent with the broader cumulative-advantage tradition in the sociology of science \citep{merton1968matthew,merton1988matthewii,allison1982cumulative,diprete2006cumulative}, but the present analysis identifies persistence rather than a causal cumulative-advantage mechanism.

The topic results make a different distinction. In the full joint corpus, static prevalence asymmetry is common: 173 topics are high on the research side but low on the project side, compared with only 10 in the reverse category. Yet annual onset is definable on both sides for only 29 of 886 topics. Moreover, the static asymmetry itself is composition-sensitive: under a 1:1 article/project balanced re-run of the frozen clustering protocol, the high-research/low-project excess does not persist (26 versus 32). The data therefore support only a composition-dependent descriptive pattern in pooled prevalence, not a stable structural asymmetry, and only a much narrower claim about temporal lag. In particular, low project-side prevalence should not automatically be read as evidence that training is ``behind'' research; for most topics, the annual threshold rule does not identify a comparable onset on both sides.

RTAS is useful precisely because it keeps this empirical question narrower than research or teaching quality. Human ratings, test--retest scoring, the LLM second rater, direct lexical baselines, and the BGE-M3 benchmark provide complementary evidence about the embedding layer, while aggregation and topic-threshold sensitivities make the main design choices explicit. At the same time, RTAS remains sensitive to title sparsity, portfolio breadth, organizational composition, and linkage coverage. RTAS and the scholar-level RT-score address complementary questions: RTAS measures project-to-portfolio semantic alignment, whereas the RT-score combines research and teaching performance at the individual scholar level; the two should therefore be viewed as complementary rather than substitutable indicators \citep{scafetta2025rt}. This bounded interpretation is consistent with responsible-metrics arguments that indicators should remain closely tied to the constructs and contexts they operationalize \citep{hicks2015leiden,wilsdon2015metrictide}, and with work distinguishing citation indicators from multidimensional research quality \citep{aksnes2019citations}. For scientometrics, the broader contribution is methodological: cross-lingual sentence embeddings make it possible to compare student-project content with academic-unit publication portfolios even when the two record systems are linguistically and administratively different. The same framework could be evaluated in other settings where project records and publication metadata coexist.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Observational design.} All associations, including the capacity-constraint interpretation, are non-causal. Stronger directional inference would require non-overlapping longitudinal waves and a design that separates within-unit from between-unit variation.

\item \textbf{Shared bibliographic construction.} For projects after 2020, some papers counted in an advisor's pre-project publication history within the institution-linked corpus can also contribute to the advisor-linked academic-unit reference portfolio used to compute RTAS. A leave-advisor-out sensitivity that removed focal-advisor articles attenuated the publication coefficient by approximately 16.4\% while leaving it positive and statistically significant, reducing but not eliminating concern about shared construction. The advisor-output coefficient remains an observational association, not an independent causal effect. Because RTAS reference portfolios accumulate over $[2020,t]$, both the year coefficient and advisor publication coefficient are also estimated against reference sets that grow and change in composition over time; the leave-advisor-out analysis addresses direct self-overlap but does not fully separate cohort differences from changing portfolio composition.

\item \textbf{Titles-only sparsity.} Titles are the only symmetric cross-lingual unit, but they compress semantics; alignment is measured on title distributions, not full texts.

\item \textbf{Linkage coverage.} 58.5\% of articles are advisor-matched to academic units; unmatched articles, including cases with missing affiliations or pinyin ambiguity, are excluded from academic-unit reference portfolios. Rule-based name linkage can generate both false-positive and false-negative assignments, so the direction of any resulting bias is uncertain. Author-level identity resolution (e.g., OpenAlex author IDs) remains future work. At the project level, complete-case estimation of the mixed model uses 3,231 of 3,714 projects, and the 483 excluded cases are not evenly distributed across academic units: exclusion rates are highest in humanities and social-science units with lower linkage coverage (51\% in the School of Marxism; 41\% in the School of History), and excluded projects have lower mean RTAS (0.099 vs 0.139). Complete-case exclusion may therefore underrepresent precisely those units, although the high-confidence-match sample and the other sensitivity analyses preserve all substantive conclusions. The study frame itself is linkage-defined: 173 projects under eight other organizational labels in the consolidated 48-unit registry had no advisor-linked publications and were excluded before RTAS analysis, so inference pertains to the 40 units retained in the analytic frame rather than to every labeled unit in the registry. Of these, 39 contribute at least one defined RTAS observation; one unit contributes a single project whose advisor-linked portfolio is empty, so no similarity is defined and its RTAS is missing.

\item \textbf{Single institution.} Holding one institutional setting fixed aids within-institution comparison but limits external validity; replication at universities with different disciplinary layouts is required before generalization.

\item \textbf{Prior-supervision history.} The project registry begins in 2020, so prior supervisory load is left-censored at that year; the covariate captures previously observed projects, not an advisor's full pre-2020 supervision history.

\item \textbf{Registry coverage and program tracks.} Official project-type labels exist only for 2021--2024; the 2020 cohort's track (innovation vs entrepreneurship) cannot be classified from the available administrative archive, and the archived 2022 official list contains no university-level entries. The analytic sample consequently pools both tracks (158 identifiable entrepreneurship projects, 4.3\%, which have lower RTAS on average: 0.100 vs 0.135, Cohen $d=-0.49$). Excluding these projects from the mixed model preserves every coefficient sign and significance classification (Table~S1), but a fully track-stratified treatment of the 2020 cohort is not possible from the available records. Whether the missing 2022 university-level records reflect the institutional arrangement that year or source-file coverage alone cannot be determined; a sensitivity that drops the entire 2022 cohort leaves the RQ1 tier contrasts and mixed-model conclusions unchanged (Table~S2).

\item \textbf{Within-year ordering.} RTAS reference portfolios include articles from the focal calendar year $t$. Because the analysis is annual, it does not establish whether a same-year article preceded or followed the project's approval date; RTAS should therefore be interpreted as contemporaneous annual alignment rather than strict within-year temporal precedence.

\item \textbf{Advisor-level dependence and multiple membership.} The primary mixed model accounts for clustering by academic unit but does not include a separate advisor random effect. A single-advisor sensitivity using academic-unit fixed effects and advisor-clustered standard errors preserved all substantive fixed-effect conclusions, including under the academic-unit $\times$ advisor homonym guard. However, 528 co-supervised projects have no primary-advisor designation, so a full multiple-membership model is not estimated.

\item \textbf{Low lag definition rate.} Only 3.27\% of topics have definable lags; LRHT/LRLT lags are undefined in this corpus, so no ``outdated training content'' claim is possible here. A curriculum-syllabus text audit could help address this gap.

\item \textbf{Measure sensitivity.} Academic-unit RTAS mixes organizational context with sensitivity to disciplinary specialization, portfolio breadth, title-language conventions, and heterogeneity; academic-unit differences should not be interpreted as performance rankings.

\item \textbf{Encoder scope.} BGE-M3 was benchmarked post-freeze on the same 150-pair reference set and did not improve pairwise validity relative to MiniLM. Because the comparison was limited to the validation set rather than a full-pipeline re-estimation, MiniLM remains the frozen primary encoder and the BGE-M3 result should be interpreted as a targeted robustness check rather than an exhaustive encoder comparison.

\item \textbf{Granularity.} $K=886$ is corpus-determined (HDBSCAN), not a theoretically fixed granularity; the topic count should not be interpreted substantively in isolation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This study introduced RTAS as a project-level, cross-lingual measure of semantic alignment between undergraduate training projects and academic-unit publication portfolios. Applied to 3,714 registered UIETP projects (3,713 with a defined RTAS) and 56,901 OpenAlex-indexed articles at Wuhan University, RTAS is structured mainly by academic unit, with about three quarters of its variance lying between units. Within that context, advisor pre-project publication activity is positively associated with alignment and prior supervisory load negatively associated; both associations persist after focal-advisor papers are removed from the reference portfolios and after within-unit-year standardization. Raw differences across administrative project tiers, by contrast, largely disappear after adjustment, and national-level project allocation is highly concentrated and persistent without a clear corresponding alignment advantage. Topic-prevalence contrasts are composition-sensitive, and temporal lags are observable for only a small subset of topics.

The contribution is therefore not a ranking of projects or academic units, but a way to observe one specific dimension of the research--training relationship: whether student-project content occupies the same semantic space as the research environment around it. This is narrower than research quality or teaching quality, but it is directly measurable and portable to other multilingual institutional settings.
